Cornell’s Future of the American University

Brief for WSU Carson College of Business

Source. Cornell University, Report of the Committee on the Future of the American University, September 22, 2026. Audience. Carson College department chairs, faculty, and the dean’s office.

This page is a discussion brief for a Carson conversation. It is not Cornell policy, and it is not a Cornell-internal process memo. It selects business-school-relevant diagnoses and recommendations. It omits Cornell’s organization chart and appendix process detail. Figures below are taken only from that report as summarized here. None are WSU or Carson statistics.

Through-line for Carson

Universities exist to cultivate sound judgment—slow, evidence-based discernment under uncertainty—not the appearance of learning that AI and résumé-signaling can fake. Use Cornell as a peer-institution mirror: where do we already build judgment, and where are we still optimizing for signals?

For reading aloud

Cornell’s September 2026 committee report argues that U.S. universities face simultaneous pressure from AI, strained federal partnerships, and falling public trust—and that the right response is to recenter education on sound judgment, not transactional credentials.

For undergraduates, it diagnoses a “transactional” model—grades, résumé lines, first-job ROI—that undercuts the risk-taking employers need, and recommends whole-person, project-based learning at human scale, AI judgment across the curriculum, and honest attention to cost and admissions incentives.

For graduate and professional programs, it stresses lifelong learning, industry pathways for most Ph.D.s, tighter master’s governance, stackable microcredentials (including applied AI), and coordination across siloed programs—directly relevant to MBA/MS design.

Research faces funding volatility; diversify support without surrendering independence, use AI responsibly, and reform publishing and open science. Public impact—land-grant-style two-way engagement with communities and employers—is the strongest remaining reservoir of public goodwill.

Leaders should treat academic freedom, shared governance, and “academic civics” as institutional capacity. Caution flags include online-only undergraduate education, compressing the degree as knowledge transfer, a rigid campus-wide core, or swinging purely vocational or purely “back to liberal arts.”

Carson’s move: use this as a peer-institution mirror—where do we already build judgment, and where are we still optimizing for signals?

Why this matters to a public business school

Cornell’s Committee on the Future of the American University—convened in September 2025, reporting September 22, 2026—engaged 6,000+ stakeholders across 250+ meetings. It issued recommendations on undergraduate education, research, public impact, graduate and professional education, and governance.

Written from Cornell’s hybrid Ivy and land-grant vantage, the report is framed as a contribution to the American university, not only to Cornell. For Carson—a public business college with undergraduate, MBA/MS, research, and regional engagement missions—it is a peer map of pressures (AI, federal strain, trust, cost, demographics) and of recommendations that sit on curriculum, professional programs, employer partnerships, and land-grant-style public value.

  • Judgment over credentials. The through-line is sound judgment, not the appearance of learning.
  • Three charged disruptors, plus more. The charge focused on AI, university–federal strain, and eroding public trust. Stakeholder work also surfaced demographics, publishing-market failure, contingent faculty, mental health, and credentialing alternatives.
  • Trust is split. Confidence in higher education has fallen, while trust in what universities do locally—economy, community, health—remains much higher.
  • Undergraduate diagnosis: transactional versus transformative. Tuition → credential → first job optimizes for signals and underprepares graduates. Transformative education compounds across careers and civic life.
  • AI is a curriculum problem, not only a tool problem. Overreliance produces the form of knowledge without inquiry. Universities should teach judgment about and beyond AI inside majors, not only in literacy modules.
  • Cost and opaque pricing. “High sticker / high aid” feeds distrust and a consumer mindset that crowds out exploration, even when average net prices are lower than stickers.
  • Admissions escalation. Common App volume, prestige via low admit rates, and AP and credential arms races train students in branding before they arrive.
  • Graduate and professional education. Most Ph.D.s no longer enter academia. Master’s growth needs real governance. Lifelong and modular pathways matter. AI hits law, business, finance, and computer science especially hard.
  • Research. The federal partnership is under severe strain. Diversify funding without becoming a contractor. Prune deliberately. Confront publishing oligopoly and reproducibility.
  • Public impact is the trust bridge. Two-way community and business engagement, not messaging alone—and reward it in faculty evaluation.
  • Governance. Defend academic freedom and tenure as public goods. Rebuild “academic civics” so shared governance is not performative.
  • Caution. Online-only undergraduate education, degree-shortening as knowledge compression, a mandated core, and pure vocationalization are not silver bullets.

Land-grant framing for Carson

The report was written from a hybrid Ivy and land-grant vantage and treats positive public impact as central to the university’s future, not a soft add-on. Carson can read each recommendation cluster through Washington State University’s land-grant pillars: teaching and learning, research and discovery, extension and engagement, and access and service to Washington.

Nothing in this section is a Carson performance claim. The report does not measure WSU. The mapping below translates the report’s undergraduate, graduate, research, and public-impact themes into the work a public business college is already charged to do.

Pillar

Teaching / Learning

Whole-person, project-based learning at human scale, with judgment about and beyond AI inside majors and professional cores. Degrees are a foundation for lifelong learning, not the endpoint. High-touch mentorship and cohorts are the differentiator when AI can cheapen some credentials.

Pillar

Research / Discovery

Keep scholarly independence while federal support is volatile. Diversify funding with safeguards. Prefer deliberate pruning to indiscriminate cuts. Use AI in research with literacy, ethics, and a distinction between human-led and AI-led work. Value public impact and entrepreneurship alongside excellence.

Pillar

Extension / Engagement

The report’s public-impact chapter is land-grant in substance: two-way relationships with communities and employers, translation of research into practice, and extension-like models for AI literacy serving the workforce, K–12, and communities. Reward that work in hiring and promotion.

Pillar

Access & service to Washington

A public college serves families who meet opaque national pricing narratives, and employers who need people who can judge under uncertainty. The report’s cost, admissions, lifelong-learning, and engagement themes are the access questions—without inventing a Washington price or outcome.

How Carson can advance the land-grant purpose

Five moves follow directly from the report’s public-impact, undergraduate, graduate, and research chapters.

  • Washington employers. Public impact, in the report, is co-creation with employers, not a messaging campaign. Carson’s analogs in the brief are corporate engagement and entrepreneurship as infrastructure, advisory boards, community research committees, and local and regional firms as labs for two-way work. New master’s and certificate programs are asked to carry a business case, break-even enrollment, and industry validation.
  • Lifelong learning. Graduate principles treat the degree as a foundation. The report’s own illustration is a 2000 MBA, which could not have prepared graduates for social media, cloud computing, algorithmic trading, and generative AI. Stackable microcredentials—including applied AI—and hybrid on-ramps and off-ramps are the proposed response, aimed at executive and alumni upskilling as well as degrees.
  • Applied research. The research and public-impact chapters ask universities to translate findings into toolkits, policy, educator materials, and incubators, not only white papers, and to diversify support—state, foundation, corporate, philanthropic—without becoming a contractor. Corporate replacement of federal support, the report notes, tends to yield less public spillover. Applied work keeps independence, integrity, and intellectual property.
  • Two-way community and business engagement. The strongest remaining public goodwill, in the report, is local economic and community trust. Leaning away from impact worsens the trust problem. Engagement is recognized in evaluation, coordinated across units without crushing bottom-up innovation, and measured as outcomes rather than activity counts. The brief asks what a Carson inventory would show for regional economic development and for domains such as family business, tribal enterprise, agribusiness, and healthcare management.
  • AI judgment for the public workforce. Undergraduate and professional chapters treat AI as a curriculum problem: when to use it, how to verify, and when not to defer, inside majors and MBA cores. Public impact adds extension-like AI literacy for workforce, K–12, and communities. Together that is preparation for public work, not an optional literacy add-on. The report is explicit that some professional credentials may get cheaper, which raises the premium on high-touch apprenticeship in judgment.

What’s driving the crisis

Teaching, research, who is served, funding, and public legitimacy are stressed together—unlike earlier disruptions that hit one dimension at a time.

Disruption themes as the committee report emphasizes them.
Theme Report emphasis
AI / tech Promise—discovery, access, translation—alongside hollowed learning, integrity problems, labor uncertainty, and environmental and equity concerns.
Federal compact The post–World War II research and student-aid partnership is contracting. Legislative pressure reaches teaching and research. Independence is at risk.
Public trust A multi-year decline. Economic grievances (price, debt, opaque ROI) and cultural grievances (conformity and viewpoint concerns the committee takes seriously without endorsing every critique).
Demographics & economics An approaching college-age “cliff,” wage and ROI anxiety, and operating models built for stable federal support.
Structural Publishing concentration, the shift from tenure-track to contingent faculty, erosion of shared governance, and alternative credentials.

The committee’s stance: these pressures will not self-resolve with the next administration or the next mood cycle. Adapt by reimagining how purpose is fulfilled—neither nostalgia nor reinvention to the latest fad.

Undergraduate education

Diagnosis

Undergraduate education has drifted toward a transactional logic: pay tuition, receive knowledge and a credential, exchange them for a job. That logic:

  • Punishes intellectual risk—hard courses, unfamiliar majors, productive failure.
  • Encourages minimizing time on campus (paths of three to three-and-a-half years) when residential time is part of the transformative offer.
  • Overweights first job and starting salary, though careers change repeatedly and later employers care about performance, not the diploma.
  • Is uniquely vulnerable to AI. If the product is “write a memo” or “produce code,” employers can buy that from machines. The defensible human seat is deciding what and why, and negotiating those choices with other people.

Universities co-produce the problem through admissions signaling, high sticker prices, and research-university incentives that undervalue teaching. Cornell context, not a Carson figure: in fall 2025, 49% of undergraduate courses and 38% of graduate courses were taught by non-tenure-track faculty.

Capacities to cultivate

  • Sound judgment
  • Work across differences
  • Civic-mindedness
  • Meaning-making
  • Connection
  • Pluralism
  • Leadership
  • Resilience
  • Flexibility
  • Courage
  • Self-knowledge

Recommendations Carson can translate

  • Whole-person education inside the curriculum, not only in student-affairs silos. Faculty and staff as co-educators.
  • Judgment about and beyond AI across majors, with discipline-specific norms. Ethics and societal implications inside majors, not only in parallel electives.
  • Hands-on, project-based pedagogy at human scale. Experiential learning—internships, co-ops, civic service—as structured practice, not résumé padding.
  • Alumni in the classroom as connectors to real work constraints.
  • Teaching incentives and supports aligned with transformative aims, including longer-term security for teaching faculty so they are not trapped in a customer-service model.
  • Technology use as an institutional and instructor decision, including when not to use devices or AI. Design spaces for relationship-rich learning.
  • Admissions. Recast messaging around preparedness, educational match, and community responsibility, not maximal accomplishment. More evidence-backed transparency. Confront credential escalation and the legitimacy costs of special-case preferences.
  • Price and cost. High stickers versus lower average net prices remain poorly understood. Opaque aid timing erodes trust and burdens middle-income families. The report recommends a tuition-model rethink, predictability (ideally, cost known at or before application), internal cost literacy, and advocacy on regulatory cost.

National price context from the report, not WSU prices. About two-thirds of adults in 2026 say four-year colleges are unaffordable. Private nonprofit sticker price is about $60,920 for 2025–26. Median household income is about $84,000.

Graduate and professional education

The chapter is directly relevant to MBA and MS design: lifelong learning, industry pathways, tighter master’s governance, and stackable credentials that include applied AI.

Principles

  • Scholarly independence.
  • Responsiveness to public and professional needs.
  • Judgment under uncertainty.
  • A global vision.
  • “Thick” relationships beyond the credential.
  • Lifelong learning: degrees as a foundation, not an endpoint.

The report’s explicit MBA-era example: a 2000 MBA could not have prepared graduates for social media, cloud computing, algorithmic trading, and generative AI.

Disruptions

  • ROI skepticism on some master’s degrees. Aggregate master’s earnings premium about 20% versus a bachelor’s in 2022 data, with wide variation by field and institution.
  • AI is reshaping how expertise is evaluated. Apprenticeship may shrink if faculty and postdocs offload formative tasks to AI.
  • Ph.D. admissions cuts tied to funding uncertainty. In an AAU sample: −11% in 2025 and −15% for fall 2026.
  • Master’s supply has boomed nationally—about +69% programs from 2005 to 2022. Cornell master’s enrollment more than doubled from 2006 to 2025. The issue is coordination and oversight, not denial of student demand.
  • The academic job market has shifted. Industry now absorbs a plurality of new Ph.D.s. Academic placement was about 33% by 2022, compared with about 52% in 1992.

Research and scholarship

  • Federal partnership. It remains unmatched for fundamental STEM research. Freezes and uncertainty expose over-reliance. Diversify—state, foundation, corporate, philanthropy—with safeguards for independence, integrity, and intellectual property. Corporate replacement of federal support tends to yield less public spillover.
  • Deliberate pruning of the research enterprise rather than indiscriminate cutting. Sharpen quality over quantity. Use internal competitive funds for high priorities where external support wanes.
  • Institutional ideas in the report include internal cross-college “moonshot” teams, discovery and creativity foundations on the WARF model, focused research-organization partnerships, and promotion standards that value entrepreneurship and public impact alongside excellence.
  • AI in research covers domain use, studying AI’s effects, literacy, ethics, and governance, distinguishing human-led from AI-led output, and two-way public engagement. Risks include “AI slop,” uneven access, privacy, energy, and research-security tensions.
  • Open science and publishing. Reproducibility concerns, adversarial collaboration, and a culture that does not punish corrections or null results. A few commercial publishers dominate—about 70% of publications from four houses in 2025. Costs outpace inflation while library investment has fallen. Options named: university-controlled open-access infrastructure, society journals, and promotion less dependent on impact-factor placement.

Public impact and engagement

Positive public impact is central to universities’ future under uncertainty. Local economic and community trust remains the strongest reservoir of goodwill. Leaning away from impact worsens the trust problem.

Imperatives in the report

  • Translate research into practice: toolkits, policy, educator materials, incubators—not only white papers.
  • Build two-way relationships. Co-create with communities and employers. Do not treat engagement as extractive “outreach.”
  • Coordinate across units without crushing bottom-up innovation.
  • Recognize and reward engagement, including entrepreneurship, in hiring and promotion.
  • Measure impact thoughtfully.
  • Use engagement infrastructure for emerging needs, including AI literacy for the workforce, K–12, and communities through extension-like models.

Angles the brief already draws for Carson

  • Corporate engagement and entrepreneurship as public-impact infrastructure.
  • Advisory boards and community research committees.
  • Local and regional firms as labs for two-way engagement.
  • An inventory of what Carson already does for Washington businesses and workers—and which outcomes can be shown beyond activity counts.

Governance, academic freedom, and civics

  • Academic freedom as a condition of public trust in learning—the AAUP tradition of unbiased inquiry rather than proprietary capture—not a private perk. Tenure as an institutional investment in future promise and independence.
  • Leaders should defend freedom when politics targets classrooms or research. Faculty must uphold professional standards: expertise, accuracy, and judgment, not an absolutist “anything goes.”
  • Academic civics: shared literacy about finance, governance, and mutual obligations, so shared governance is not hollow. Complementary to governance mechanics, not identical with them.
  • Shared governance as institutional judgment across constituencies.

For Carson. Secure pathways for teaching and career faculty. Treat service as civic participation in evaluation. Join cross-college chair forums that include business with other social sciences.

Roads not taken

The undergraduate chapter warns against pathways that look like fixes for cost or relevance and undermine transformative aims.

  1. Online-only undergraduate education as the model

    Online can work for targeted professional upskilling and for some master’s programs. It poorly develops judgment, work across difference, and resilience, which require being known and making mistakes in shared presence.

  2. Shortening the path to a degree as pure compression

    Distinguish time-to-degree from time-for-growth. Acceleration that assumes education is knowledge transfer is flawed for most 18-year-olds. Exceptions named in the report include prior military or associate pathways and carefully designed 3+1 routes.

  3. A unified core curriculum

    A mandated core risks outsourcing whole-person aims to a checklist and encouraging transactional completion. The report prefers shared competency frameworks implemented inside college curricula. A decentralized college structure is treated as a feature.

  4. Becoming more vocational only

    Vocational training and transformative residential education are complementary, not interchangeable. Pure vocationalism risks obsolescence when AI shifts practice, and it underprepares people for cross-boundary leadership.

  5. Becoming less vocational, or an unchanged “return” to the liberal arts

    The liberal arts have to be reinvigorated as judgment practice—unsettling questions, weighing, choosing—not restated as critical-thinking marketing or as delivery of beliefs. Business cases can teach analysis too. The distinctive liberal-arts aim is character and judgment under uncertainty.

  6. Broader cautions

    Do not replace the federal partnership with corporate capture. Do not grow master’s programs without governance. Do not cut teaching capacity without pedagogical analysis. Balance coordination with program autonomy so students experience one university, not a federation of silos.

Discussion questions for Carson

  1. Where does our undergraduate business curriculum still optimize for credential signaling—GPA, internship count, club offices—at the expense of judgment under ambiguity, and what would transformative assessment look like in core courses?
  2. How should Carson embed AI judgment—when to use it, how to verify, when not to defer—inside majors and MBA cores rather than as an optional literacy add-on?
  3. Which MBA and MS differentiators are high-touch apprenticeship in judgment, and which offerings are vulnerable to cheaper online or AI substitutes?
  4. Do we have a real business-case and employer-validation process for new master’s degrees and certificates, including break-even enrollment and pricing transparency?
  5. How should Carson partner with Washington employers on lifelong and stackable learning—applied AI, analytics, leadership communication—without diluting degree quality?
  6. In promotion and annual review, how do we weight teaching excellence, public and business engagement, and entrepreneurship relative to traditional research counts, especially for career-track faculty?
  7. What would a Carson public-impact inventory show for regional economic development, family business, tribal enterprise, agribusiness, healthcare management, and similar domains—and which outcomes can we measure beyond activities?
  8. Given opaque national pricing narratives, how do we communicate net cost, ROI, and residential or cohort value to Washington families without pure transactional marketing—and how should admissions and scholarships recruit for curiosity and contribution, not maximal branding?
  9. Which “roads not taken” are we tempted by locally—fully online undergraduate expansion, compressed degrees, vocational-only pivots—and what evidence from this report should constrain those choices?
